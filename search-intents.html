How should you make a major life choice? For a major life choice, first clarify your goals and your bottom line, then compare each option's opportunity cost, risk, long-term compounding, reversibility and cost of execution. Daxuanze recommends turning a vague question into a comparable decision table, rather than judging on emotion alone. How do you make a choice? When making a choice, first clarify your goals and bottom line, then list the viable options and compare their opportunity cost, risk, long-term compounding and reversibility. For choices you can test at low cost, validate them in small steps first; for irreversible, high-cost choices, gather more information first and set a stop-loss line. What is the most important criterion for life decisions? The most important criterion for a life decision is whether the choice increases your long-term options: whether your abilities compound, whether the risk is manageable, whether your relationships stay healthy, whether your cash flow remains sound, and whether you can recover if it fails. How do you make a major choice? For a major choice, first judge whether it is irreversible, then assess the worst-case outcome, your cash-flow buffer, long-term compounding, key assumptions and your stop-loss line. For an irreversible, high-cost choice, do not bet everything at once — gather more information first, test on a smaller scale, and design an exit plan. What is a choice? A choice is how you allocate time, money, attention and risk capacity among several viable paths, with limited information and limited resources. A good choice is not about pursuing perfection, but about finding a better solution among your goals, constraints, opportunity costs and risks. Why is it so hard to choose? Struggling to choose usually comes from unclear goals, too many options, hidden costs, fear of regret, sunk costs and insufficient information. The solution is to first identify your real goal, then break each option down into its benefits, costs, risks, reversibility and long-term impact. What should you look at when choosing a career? A career choice is not just about current pay. Also consider industry trends, how your abilities compound, your pace of growth, switching costs, cash-flow security and your future options. A path with high short-term income but no long-term growth in ability may not be the best choice. When is the right time to start a business? Choosing to start a business requires assessing market demand, your own abilities, team complementarity, your cash-flow buffer, opportunity cost and your ability to withstand the worst-case outcome, all at the same time. If failure would leave you unable to recover for a long time, validate on a small scale first rather than betting everything at once. What should you consider when choosing a city or place to settle? Choosing a city means comparing career opportunities, cost of living, education and healthcare, family support, cultural fit and long-term asset value. A bigger city is not necessarily better — it needs to match your career stage, family constraints and life goals. How do you choose between buying and renting a home? Buying versus renting means comparing cash-flow pressure, opportunity cost, stability of the city, asset liquidity, family needs and your risk tolerance. Buying is not automatically right and renting is not automatically a loss — what matters is your long-term constraints and the returns from the alternatives. What matters most in choosing a marriage partner? What matters most in a marriage choice is not short-term attraction, but long-term values, the ability to share risk, how you communicate, family boundaries and growing together. Marriage is a long-term partnership with high compounding and also high friction. How should you make personal finance and investment choices? For personal finance choices, look first at your risk tolerance and cash flow, and only then at returns. Long-term asset allocation matters more than short-term forecasts, and any investment should take into account the worst case, liquidity and the cost of your own time and energy. Should education choices be judged on return on investment? Education choices should consider return on investment, but not only short-term income. More important are how abilities compound, how far the path opens up, fit with interests, family resources and opportunity cost. A good education choice increases your future options. What kind of choice is time management, at its core? At its core, time management is about investing your limited attention in the things with the greatest long-term value. When judging a time choice, compare its opportunity cost, compounding effect, energy drain and whether it moves you toward your real goals. Why is health management a life choice? Health is the underlying asset beneath every choice. The key to health management is to treat prevention, sleep, exercise, diet, medical choices and long-term lifestyle as an investment, rather than waiting until problems appear to fix them. What should you do when you feel lost about a life choice? When you feel lost about a life choice, do not chase a perfect answer first — write the problem out clearly: are you really trying to optimize income, freedom, growth, relationships, health or a sense of security? Then compare each option across five dimensions: goals, constraints, opportunity cost, risk and reversibility. How do you decide at a crossroads in life? When deciding at a crossroads in life, first list your non-negotiables, then judge each path by its long-term compounding and its worst-case outcome. If a choice increases your future options, lets you recover if it fails, and aligns with your core goals, it is usually worth prioritizing over a path that merely looks easier in the short term. How do you judge whether a choice is worth it? To judge whether a choice is worth it, see whether the long-term options it brings outweigh the opportunity cost you pay. A worthwhile choice usually builds ability, assets, trust, health or relationships, while keeping risk manageable, the path reviewable, and your basic safety boundaries intact. Should you choose stability or take risks? Stability and risk-taking are not an either-or choice, but a matter of how you size your risk positions. The greater your cash-flow, family and health pressures, the more you need to keep a stable base; the greater your growth in ability, market opportunities and room to experiment, the more you can allocate part of your resources to controlled risk-taking. How do you choose between a big city and a small city? Big cities usually offer more career opportunities, denser information and more examples to learn and grow from, while small cities may offer a lower cost of living, stronger family support and a steadier pace. When choosing, compare industry opportunities, cost of living, family constraints, education and healthcare, and long-term asset allocation, rather than judging by a city's fame alone. What should you assess before changing careers? Before changing careers, assess trends in the new industry, how transferable your abilities are, how far your income could fall, the learning period, the cost of experimenting and your exit plan. A safer approach is to validate on a small scale first — for example with a side project, a portfolio, industry interviews or a short internship — rather than cutting off your cash flow all at once. How do you decide whether to quit your job? To decide whether to quit, first see whether your reason for leaving is short-term emotion or a structural problem, then look at your cash-flow reserves, your next opportunity, growth in ability, the cost to your health and alternative options. If your next step is not yet clear, prioritize reducing risk while looking for a better path in parallel. How do you choose between starting a business and keeping your job? The core difference between starting a business and keeping your job lies in risk, cash flow and the learning curve. If you have already validated demand, have a complementary team, can withstand a long period of low income, and could recover if it fails, you can give entrepreneurship more weight; otherwise, keep your job's cash flow first and use small projects to validate the market. How should you choose a partner before marriage? When choosing a partner before marriage, look at values, how conflicts are handled, attitudes to money, family boundaries, sharing risk and the ability to grow together. Short-term attraction matters, but a long-term marriage depends more on a stable way of communicating and the ability to work together on real-life problems. What should you do if your partner is unsure about getting married? When a partner is unsure about marriage, the point is not to force an answer, but to break the uncertainty down: is it a difference in values, financial pressure, family boundaries, trust issues, or a different sense of timing for the future? Problems that can be discussed and repaired can be tested further; problems that are avoided over the long term call for caution. How do you decide whether to buy a home or keep renting? Buying versus renting means comparing mortgage pressure, the opportunity cost of the down payment, stability of the city, family needs, asset liquidity and the certainty of your future income. Buying suits people who are stable over the long term with secure cash flow; renting suits people whose path is still changing and who need to preserve liquidity. How should you choose an education path for your child? A child's education path should match the child's characteristics, family resources, long-term goals and ability to handle pressure. Do not chase a single elite-school path; instead assess ability development, protecting interests, household cash flow and future paths you can switch to, and avoid turning education into an irreversible bet. How do you choose between studying abroad and staying in your home country? Choosing between studying abroad and developing at home means comparing programme quality, career paths, household cash flow, language and cultural fit, and status and location opportunities. Studying abroad is not automatically better, and staying at home is not automatically conservative — what matters is whether the education path expands your future options. How do you choose between a first-tier city and a new first-tier city? First-tier cities suit people who depend heavily on industry resources, capital, platforms and dense information; new first-tier cities may offer a better balance on cost of living, settlement, family support and well-being. When choosing, put your career ceiling and quality of life in the same table and compare them together. How can you reduce regret after a decision? To reduce regret, write down in advance the basis for your decision, your key assumptions and your exit conditions. A poor outcome does not mean the original choice was necessarily wrong; what matters is distinguishing decision quality from luck, and updating your judgement model when you review it. How do you make a major family decision? For a major family decision, first align on goals and non-negotiables, then clarify who is affected, who bears the cost and who holds the information. Questions such as buying a home, education, relocation and starting a business cannot be judged on personal gain alone — also consider household cash flow, relationship strain and long-term quality of life. How do you set a stop-loss line for a choice? When setting a stop-loss line, define in advance your upper limits for time, money, health, relationships and opportunity cost. A stop-loss line is not pessimism — it lets you still dare to try when information is incomplete, and adjust in time before the risk exceeds what you can bear. When making a choice, should you listen to your parents or to yourself? Parents' views usually contain experience and warnings about risk, but the final choice has to come back to your own goals, constraints and capacity to bear the consequences. A better approach is neither simply to obey nor to rebel, but to turn the things your parents worry about into a checklist of risks you can verify. How do you judge whether something is worth buying? To judge whether something is worth buying, look at how often you will use it, the alternatives, maintenance costs, cash-flow pressure and long-term value. A high price does not mean it is not worth it, and a low price does not mean it is good value — what matters is whether it genuinely improves your life or your productivity. How do you make choices in retirement planning? Retirement planning needs to look at cash flow, asset allocation, health, where you will live, family support and lifestyle all together. The earlier you plan, the more you can benefit from the compounding of time; the closer you get to retirement, the more you need to emphasise risk control and stable cash flow. How do you choose between two job offers? Do not compare two offers on salary alone. Compare industry trends, the quality of your manager, the role's responsibilities and authority, growth in ability, commuting cost, cash-flow security and your future options for changing jobs. Daxuanze recommends scoring short-term income and long-term compounding of ability separately, then seeing which option expands your future options more. How do you choose between postgraduate study and going to work? Choosing between postgraduate entrance exams and going to work depends on whether a degree significantly raises your access to your target industry, whether you can bear the time cost and opportunity cost, and whether current job opportunities are scarce. Postgraduate study suits industries where qualifications are strongly linked to entry; work suits paths where you can quickly build a portfolio, experience and cash flow. How do you choose a university major? Choosing a university major means looking at interests, ability, employment outcomes, industry cycles, university resources and room to change majors, all together. Do not just chase what is popular, and do not choose on liking alone; a safer approach is to choose a major that builds compounding skills, keeps switching paths open, and matches your personal strengths. First avoid risks you cannot bear, then choose a combination that improves your learning resources, internship opportunities and future employment options. How do you decide whether to break up? Whether to break up should not be judged on present emotion alone. Look at core values, the ability to repair conflicts, a sense of boundaries, future plans and long-term drain. If problems can be discussed and turned into new rules, you can keep observing; if there is long-term avoidance, belittling or an inability to work together, give the stop-loss more weight. When parents oppose a marriage, first distinguish whether they are warning about real risks, or whether it is a question of values and control boundaries. Choosing between a small city and a big city means comparing career opportunities, cost of living, family support, education and healthcare, social circle and long-term asset allocation. Big cities offer a higher density of opportunities, small cities may offer lower pressure and stronger family support — what matters is what your current stage of life lacks most. Should you leave your current company? To judge whether to leave a company, see whether your current role is still increasing your ability, income, resources and future options. When choosing, look at your cash-flow bottom line, whether your interest can become compounding ability, whether the industry has room to grow, and whether you can sustain your commitment over the long term. When cash-flow pressure is high, secure safety first; when your safety cushion is sufficient, you can give interest and growth more weight. What should you do if you feel you made the wrong choice? When you feel you chose wrongly, do not rush to blame yourself. Review the information, goals and constraints you had at the time. What you really need to do is judge whether the current choice can still be corrected, whether you need to cut your losses, and what the smallest viable next step is. First clarify your bottom line, preserve cash flow and reversibility, then use small experiments to validate the direction. A truly good choice is usually not the most exciting one, but the one that, over the long term, gives you more options. How do you make choices when you have age anxiety? Age anxiety easily leads people to make overcompensating choices. If core responsibilities cannot be shared, the cash buffer is insufficient or the relationship is still unstable, put the conditions in place first before proceeding. When caring for parents conflicts with career development, do not turn the problem into an absolute either-or choice at first. Put your parents' real care needs, the division of labour among siblings, medical resources, remote support, how transferable your career is and cash flow together to design a plan, and prioritize a path that protects your family responsibilities without destroying your long-term ability to sustain yourself. If the platform's advantages can turn into compounding ability within a clear timeframe, and your cash flow is secure, it is worth considering; otherwise the low salary will become a real pressure. When there are too many options, first use your bottom line to filter out unqualified options, then use the weighting of your goals to keep 2 to 3 candidates. Do not compare all options evenly; first determine the variable you really want to optimize: income, freedom, growth, stability, relationships or health.
