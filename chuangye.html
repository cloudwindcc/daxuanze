When is the right time to start a business? Choosing to start a business requires assessing market demand, your own abilities, team complementarity, your cash-flow buffer, opportunity cost and your ability to withstand the worst-case outcome, all at the same time. If failure would leave you unable to recover for a long time, validate on a small scale first rather than betting everything at once. How do you choose between starting a business and keeping your job? The core difference between starting a business and keeping your job lies in risk, cash flow and the learning curve. If you have already validated demand, have a complementary team, can withstand a long period of low income, and could recover if it fails, you can give entrepreneurship more weight; otherwise, keep your job's cash flow first and use small projects to validate the market. Should you start a business in partnership with a friend? Before starting a business with a friend, first check whether your abilities are complementary, whether your contributions are balanced, whether the decision-making mechanism is clear, whether equity has a vesting period, and whether exit rules can be written down in advance. Friendship can lower the initial cost of trust, but it cannot replace a business contract; if exit and division of labour cannot be discussed clearly, you should not partner right away. Should you find a co-founder or start a business on your own? Whether to find a co-founder depends on whether a key ability gap genuinely exists, and whether the other person can take on complementary responsibilities. Going solo suits early validation of a simple need; partnership suits projects where product, sales, technology or operations clearly complement each other. Do not partner to ease loneliness — partner to increase your chances of success. Validate With a Side Project Before Quitting to Start a Business Starting a business is not about bravely quitting; it is about breaking uncontrollable risk into small, verifiable experiments. Before Partnering in a Startup, Talk About Exit and Division of Labour First A good startup partnership is not about having a good relationship, but about being able to clarify risks, rights and responsibilities, and exit mechanisms in advance.
