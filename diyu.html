The PLACE City Choice Model

P - Position

Core question: How does this city position your career development?

Assessment factors: Industry standing, career ceiling, development path

Key indicators: Industry concentration, number of leading companies, salary levels

L - Leverage

Core question: Can this city amplify your strengths?

Assessment factors: Resource concentration, networks, platform effects

Key indicators: Population size, talent density, startup ecosystem

A - Adapt

Core question: Can you adapt to life in this city?

Assessment factors: Cultural differences, climate, pace of life

Key indicators: Dialect prevalence, climate suitability, convenience of daily life

C - Cost

Core question: What is the overall cost of this city?

Assessment factors: Cost of living, opportunity cost, relocation cost

Key indicators: Price-to-income ratio, consumption index, relocation expenses

E - Evolve

Core question: What is the future potential of this city?

Assessment factors: Policy support, industrial structure, population trends

Key indicators: GDP growth rate, net population inflow, degree of policy support

Analysis of China's City Tiers

Tier 1 cities

Representative cities: Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, Shenzhen

Strengths: Top-tier resources, high-paying opportunities, an international outlook

Weaknesses: High cost of living, fierce competition, high pressure

Best for: Top talent, entrepreneurs, internationally minded professionals

New tier 1 cities

Representative cities: Hangzhou, Chengdu, Chongqing, Wuhan

Strengths: Fast development, moderate cost of living, many opportunities

Weaknesses: Relatively narrow industry mix, rising competition

Best for: Growth-oriented talent, those seeking balance

Tier 2 cities

Representative cities: Xiamen, Qingdao, Dalian, Suzhou

Strengths: Comfortable living, lower costs, strong development potential

Weaknesses: Relatively limited resources, a lower development ceiling

Best for: Those who prioritize quality of life, steady developers

What should you consider when choosing a city or place to settle? Choosing a city means comparing career opportunities, cost of living, education and healthcare, family support, cultural fit and long-term asset value. A bigger city is not necessarily better — it needs to match your career stage, family constraints and life goals. How do you choose between a big city and a small city? Big cities usually offer more career opportunities, denser information and more examples to learn and grow from, while small cities may offer a lower cost of living, stronger family support and a steadier pace. When choosing, compare industry opportunities, cost of living, family constraints, education and healthcare, and long-term asset allocation, rather than judging by a city's fame alone. How do you choose between a first-tier city and a new first-tier city? First-tier cities suit people who depend heavily on industry resources, capital, platforms and dense information; new first-tier cities may offer a better balance on cost of living, settlement, family support and well-being. When choosing, put your career ceiling and quality of life in the same table and compare them together. Should you stay in a small city or go to a big city? Choosing between a small city and a big city means comparing career opportunities, cost of living, family support, education and healthcare, social circle and long-term asset allocation. Big cities offer a higher density of opportunities, small cities may offer lower pressure and stronger family support — what matters is what your current stage of life lacks most. Should you return to your hometown to care for your parents? Whether to return to your hometown to care for your parents means comparing your parents' health status, local medical resources, career opportunities, household cash flow and alternative care support all together. Daxuanze recommends first designing options such as remote arrangements, regular trips home, parents staying in the city for short periods, or returning in phases — and only considering a long-term return when care needs are ongoing and cannot be outsourced. What should you do when caring for parents conflicts with career development? When caring for parents conflicts with career development, do not turn the problem into an absolute either-or choice at first. Put your parents' real care needs, the division of labour among siblings, medical resources, remote support, how transferable your career is and cash flow together to design a plan, and prioritize a path that protects your family responsibilities without destroying your long-term ability to sustain yourself. Stay in a First-Tier City or Return to Your Home City A city is not better just because it is bigger; it needs to match your life stage and family constraints. Whether to Return to Your Hometown to Care for Parents: Design a Two-City Plan Caring for parents does not only have the path of sacrificing your career; a good choice should protect both family responsibility and long-term ability to sustain yourself.
