Choice Methodology

What Is a Choice: Decision Difficulty and Decision Methodology

A choice is not picking one of two answers; it is ranking your time, money, attention, relationships and risk capacity amid limited resources, limited information and an uncertain future.

A Good Choice Usually Meets 6 Conditions

Clear Goal

You know whether you are really optimizing income, freedom, health, relationships, growth or security.

Clear Constraints

You acknowledge time, cash flow, family responsibilities, the limits of your ability and the external environment.

Visible Opportunity Cost

You see what choosing A gives up, rather than only looking at the benefits of A itself.

Bearable Risk

The worst outcome will not drag you into a state you cannot recover from.

Long-Term Compounding

The choice builds ability, assets, trust, health or new options.

Reviewable

You keep the basis for your judgement, so once results appear you can calibrate your decision model.

7 Common Causes of Decision Difficulty

  1. 1. Treating other people's goals as your own.
  2. 2. Comparing only the benefits, not the costs.
  3. 3. Mistaking sunk costs for a reason to keep going.
  4. 4. Overestimating short-term emotion and underestimating long-term compounding.
  5. 5. Mixing low-probability disasters together with high-probability small losses.
  6. 6. Having no exit mechanism, which makes it hard to start.
  7. 7. Collecting too much information, but having no decision criteria.

What is a choice? A choice is how you allocate time, money, attention and risk capacity among several viable paths, with limited information and limited resources. A good choice is not about pursuing perfection, but about finding a better solution among your goals, constraints, opportunity costs and risks. How do you judge whether a choice is worth it? To judge whether a choice is worth it, see whether the long-term options it brings outweigh the opportunity cost you pay. A worthwhile choice usually builds ability, assets, trust, health or relationships, while keeping risk manageable, the path reviewable, and your basic safety boundaries intact. Should you choose stability or take risks? Stability and risk-taking are not an either-or choice, but a matter of how you size your risk positions. The greater your cash-flow, family and health pressures, the more you need to keep a stable base; the greater your growth in ability, market opportunities and room to experiment, the more you can allocate part of your resources to controlled risk-taking. How can you reduce regret after a decision? To reduce regret, write down in advance the basis for your decision, your key assumptions and your exit conditions. A poor outcome does not mean the original choice was necessarily wrong; what matters is distinguishing decision quality from luck, and updating your judgement model when you review it.
